\section{Setting}\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Six Birds in brief.} Six Birds describes how a higher-level description is held together by six primitive operations: P1 operator rewrite, P2 constraints, P3 protocol holonomy (route mismatch), P4 staging, P5 packaging, and P6 accounting \citep{tsiokos2026sixbirds}. Three of them carry most of the weight here. \emph{Packaging} (P5) decides which distinctions become objects at a layer. \emph{Staging} (P4) fixes the time scale at which those objects are expected to persist. \emph{Accounting} (P6) records that keeping objects predictive costs memory, sensing, or computation. Route mismatch (P3) reappears in \Cref{sec:diagnostics} as a diagnostic. Randomness is not a seventh primitive. In this paper, it is the unpredictability that remains when packaging, staging, and accounting leave relevant distinctions unresolved.

\paragraph{Substrate and packaging.} We work with finite state spaces throughout. This setting is already rich enough to show the phenomenon, and it matches our experiments. The substrate is a stationary process $(X_t)$ on a finite microstate space $\mathcal{X}$, with stationary one-time law $\pi$. When explicit dynamics are needed, $(X_t)$ is a time-homogeneous Markov chain with transition matrix $P$, and $\pi$ is a stationary law of $P$, so $\pi P=\pi$. A \emph{layer} is a packaging map $\Pi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}$ onto a finite set of packages, together with a staging lag $\tau\in\{1,2,\dots\}$. The packaged process is
\begin{equation}
Y_t := \Pi(X_t).
\label{eq:packaged-process}
\end{equation}
The \emph{fiber} of a package $y$ is the set $\Pi^{-1}(y)$ of microstates it groups together. A microstate $x$ is \emph{occupied} if $\pi(x)>0$, and a package is occupied if its fiber has positive total mass.

\paragraph{Conventions.} All information quantities are computed under the stationary joint law of the variables involved. Logarithms are natural, so entropies are in nats. We use $H(\cdot\mid\cdot)$ for conditional Shannon entropy, $I(\cdot\,;\cdot\mid\cdot)$ for conditional mutual information, and $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ for Kullback--Leibler divergence \citep{cover2006elementsofinformationtheory}.

\paragraph{The question.} Take two occupied microstates $x$ and $x'$ in the same package. Do they lead to the same distribution of packages $\tau$ steps later? If every such pair does, the current package is a sufficient summary for predicting the next package, and nothing hidden inside it matters. If some pair does not, the package hides a distinction that still affects the future. Someone who sees only packages will experience that hidden distinction as randomness. \Cref{sec:closure} measures exactly how much.
