\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

There are two familiar stories about randomness. In the first, randomness is a feature of the world: some outcomes are genuinely undetermined. In the second, it is a feature of the observer: the world is orderly, but we lack the data or the computing power to predict it. Both stories skip a step that every scientific description must take. No theory works with the raw substrate. It first decides which distinctions to keep as objects, such as positions, phases, cell types, or market states, and which to ignore. Whatever its chosen objects fail to predict, it then calls noise, chance, or stochasticity. A claim that something is random is therefore incomplete until we say at which level of description, under which grouping of states, and with how much memory or computation.

This paper makes that observation quantitative. We call the grouping a \emph{packaging}: a map $\Pi$ from microstates $x$ to coarse states $y=\Pi(x)$. We write $X_t$ for the microstate at time $t$ and $Y_t=\Pi(X_t)$ for the packaged state. The question we ask is simple. Once we know the current package $Y_t$, does the hidden microstate $X_t$ still tell us anything about the next package $Y_{t+\tau}$? The amount it tells us is the \emph{micro-closure deficit}
\[
\mathsf{CD}_\tau(\Pi) := I(X_t; Y_{t+\tau} \mid Y_t),
\]
a conditional mutual information. A one-line chain-rule argument gives the exact decomposition
\[
H(Y_{t+\tau}\mid Y_t)
=
H(Y_{t+\tau}\mid X_t)
+
\mathsf{CD}_\tau(\Pi).
\]
The left side is the uncertainty about the next package for someone who sees only packages. The first term on the right is the uncertainty that would remain even with the full current microstate. The second term is the extra uncertainty created by the packaging. If the dynamics are deterministic given the microstate, the first term is zero, and all of the packaged unpredictability comes from the packaging. Our thesis is that this second term is the right formal object for \emph{layer-relative randomness}: randomness that exists because of the level at which a process is described.

The idea sits inside the Six Birds program, which treats a higher-level description as a closure that must be maintained rather than as a free summary \citep{tsiokos2026sixbirds}. Earlier papers in the series supply the ingredients we use. \emph{Six Birds: Foundations of Emergence Calculus} introduced packaging as an idempotent form of closure \citep{tsiokos2026sixbirds}. \emph{To Create a Stone with Six Birds} built induced macro kernels and route-mismatch diagnostics for coarse-grained stochastic dynamics \citep{tsiokos2026pica}. \emph{To Notch a Stone with Six Birds} used a gap between first- and second-order predictive log loss to detect when a theory must be refined \citep{tsiokos2026notch}. The present paper asks what failure of closure looks like from inside a description that keeps running anyway. Our answer is that it looks like randomness.

The account also touches several external traditions. Information-bottleneck methods treat prediction as something bought under a representational budget \citep{tishby1999bottleneck,geiger2015optimalkullbackleibleraggregation}. Computational mechanics uses predictive sufficiency to separate structure from randomness \citep{shalizi2001computationalmechanics}. The theory of lumpable Markov chains asks exactly when a partition of states yields a Markov chain on its own \citep{kemeny1976finitemarkovchains,buchholz1994exactordinarylumpability}. Our emphasis is different. We treat predictive residue explicitly as the deficit of a chosen packaging, relate it to diagnostics already used in Six Birds, and carry the same logic to a deterministic many-to-one map, a cryptographic hash. Following the random-oracle tradition \citep{bellare1993randomoracles}, we use an ideal random function as the reference for what one-way behavior looks like.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The paper has a mathematical part and an experimental part.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Exact theory} (Sections~\ref{sec:closure} and~\ref{sec:diagnostics}). We define the closure deficit, prove the decomposition, and write the deficit as an average Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence over occupied microstates. We show that it vanishes exactly for closed packagings when the stationary law has full support. We prove that it bounds the squared stationary route mismatch, and that, for Markov substrates, it bounds the predictive gain available from any finite window of packaged history. We also record what does \emph{not} hold in general. Finite counterexamples show that the support condition cannot be dropped, that the route-mismatch bound fails for a uniform lift, and that the deficit need not shrink with longer lags or finer packagings.
\item \emph{Controlled demonstrations} (\Cref{sec:results}). On a seventeen-condition Markov benchmark, the deficit is zero for lumpable partitions, grows with built-in heterogeneity, and is strongly correlated with route mismatch. In a memory-budget benchmark, exact population entropies show how much of the deficit packaged memory can recover. Validation-selected predictors trained on finite data reproduce the first two steps of that descent. In a toy hashing experiment, high-entropy inputs make truncated SHA-256 behave like an ideal random function under bounded search, while low-entropy inputs make inversion easy for an attacker who knows their source.
\end{enumerate}
A small Lean development (\Cref{app:lean}) machine-checks the finite KL identities behind the expected-divergence form.

\paragraph{Scope.} We do not claim that all randomness reduces to hidden microstructure. When the substrate itself is stochastic, the intrinsic term is real and is not touched by our analysis. We also do not identify closure deficit with irreversibility or with the birth of new theory. Our claim is narrower and checkable: once a substrate, a packaging, a time scale, and a memory budget are fixed, the part of packaged randomness caused by the packaging is a well-defined quantity that can be computed exactly in finite models and approximated by observable diagnostics.
